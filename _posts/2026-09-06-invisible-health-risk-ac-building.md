I walk out of my AC lab six times a day. The lab sits at 18°C. Chennai outside is 37°C. That's a 19-degree jump — and my body absorbs it in the ten seconds it takes to reach the parking lot.

By evening, I'm tired. Sometimes I have a headache. Sometimes my throat feels scratchy. I used to blame it on sleep, or the hostel food, or just "college stress." Turns out the actual cause might be something nobody around me was tracking — and something I never even thought of as a health risk.

---

## What's actually happening to your body

When you step from an 18°C room into 37°C heat, your blood vessels rapidly dilate. Your blood pressure drops. Your respiratory system goes from cold, dry air to hot, humid air in seconds. Your body's thermoregulation system — the internal machinery that keeps you at 37°C — suddenly has to work overtime.

Do this once, and you barely notice. Do it six times a day, five days a week, for months — and it compounds into something researchers call **thermal transition stress**.

I'm not making this up. This isn't a wellness blog theory. This is published, peer-reviewed research.

---

## The research that changed how I think about AC

A [study from AIIMS Hyderabad](https://journals.lww.com/ijoe/fulltext/2023/27010/impact_of_air_conditioners_on_sick_building.6.aspx) tracked 400 adults and found that people who frequently switch between air-conditioned indoor environments and outdoor heat experience what they called "thermal regulation stress due to disturbance in thermal adaptation and reduced tolerance to hot environments."

Read that last part again — **reduced tolerance to hot environments.** The AC isn't just cooling you. It's training your body to handle heat worse.

A separate [study published in ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/0304400977900031) found that even healthy individuals accustomed to air conditioning showed "substantial thermal burdening when abruptly exposed to urban exterior environments." These weren't sick people. These were healthy office workers — and the transition alone was enough to measurably stress their bodies.

And here's the one that really hit me — a [study on office workers in Malaysia](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12553057/) found that AC users consistently reported higher rates of Sick Building Syndrome symptoms:

- **47.6%** reported headaches
- **44.4%** reported cough
- **38.7%** had nasal irritation
- **25%** experienced fatigue
- **25%** had dizziness

Almost half. Getting headaches. From the building they work in.

---

## The threshold nobody told us about

There's a [recommended safe limit](https://www.holtop.com/news/air-conditioning-and-heatstrokeheat-shock-response) for the temperature difference between indoor and outdoor environments — **10°C**.

At my campus, the gap is **20°C**. Double the safe threshold.

And I experience this gap six times a day. Monday through Friday. For the entire semester.

When I first calculated this, I sat with it for a minute. I'd been living inside a health risk I could literally feel — the fatigue, the throat issues, the weekly colds — but I never connected it to the one thing I do dozens of times a week: walking in and out of AC.

---

## The scale of this problem

This isn't just a VIT Chennai problem.

- India has over **40 million** office workers in air-conditioned environments
- There are more than **30,000 colleges** with AC classrooms in tropical climates
- The WHO reports that Sick Building Syndrome [affects over 20% of building occupants globally](https://academic.oup.com/ijlct/article/doi/10.1093/ijlct/ctae191/7823455)
- Chennai specifically has a **15–22°C** indoor-outdoor gap for eight months of the year

And yet — I searched for a tool that tracks this. An app. A wearable feature. A campus system. Anything.

**Nothing exists.**

There are temperature apps. There are weather apps. There are indoor air quality monitors. But not a single product tracks what actually matters — how many times you transition between thermal zones in a day, how big each jump is, and what that cumulative exposure does to your health.

---

## Why I started paying attention

I'm a first-year CSE student. I'm not a doctor or a climate scientist. But I can read research papers, and I can count.

Six transitions a day. 19-degree average gap. Five days a week. That's roughly **570 degrees of cumulative thermal shock per week** that my body is absorbing — and I was blaming the headaches on not drinking enough water.

This is the kind of problem that hides in plain sight. Everyone experiences it. Nobody measures it. And because nobody measures it, nobody fixes it — not the student, not the university, not the AC system.

I started thinking about what it would take to actually track this. Not with expensive sensors or medical devices — just with the phone in my pocket, a weather API, and some basic math.

That became an idea worth exploring. More on the technical side in the next post.

---

## The uncomfortable question

Next time you walk out of your AC office or classroom into the heat — and you feel that wave hit you — ask yourself:

**How many times did I do this today?**

And then ask:

**Is anyone counting?**

Because the research says it matters. And right now, the answer is nobody.
